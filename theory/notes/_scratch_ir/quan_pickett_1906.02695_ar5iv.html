How Compressed Hydrides Produce Room Temperature Superconductivity

Yundi Quan, Soham S. Ghosh, and Warren E. Pickett Affiliation: University of California Davis, Davis CA 95616 Email: pickett@physics.ucdavis.edu
August 8, 2026
Abstract

The 2014-2015 prediction, discovery, and confirmation of record high temperature superconductivity above 200K in H3S, followed by the 2018 extension to superconductivity in the 250-280K range in lanthanum hydride, marks a new era in the longstanding quest for room temperature superconductivity: quest achieved, at the cost of supplying 1.5-2 megabars of pressure. Predictions of numerous high temperature superconducting metal hydrides X​HnXH_{n} (XX=metal) have appeared, but are providing limited understanding of what drives the high transition temperature Tc, or what limits Tc. We apply an opportunistic atomic decomposition of the coupling function to show, first, that the XX atom provides coupling strength as commonly calculated, but is it irrelevant for superconductivity; in fact, it is important for analysis that its contribution is neglected. Five XXHn compounds, predicted to have Tc in the 150-300K range, are analyzed consistently for their relevant properties, revealing some aspects that confront conventional wisdom. A phonon frequency – critical temperature (ω2\omega_{2}-Tc) phase diagram is obtained that reveals a common phase instability limiting Tc at the low pressure range of each compound. The hydrogen scattering strength is identified and found to differ strongly over the hydrides. A quantity directly proportional to Tc in these hydrides is identified.

I Background

The disruptive discovery of record high temperature superconductivity (HTS) in SH3 above 200KDuan2014; Drozdov2015; Einaga2016; Duan2017 has now been superseded by reports from two groups of critical temperatures Tc in the 250-280K range in lanthanum hydride,ME2018a; MS2019; ME2018b both requiring pressure in the 160-190 GPa range. The mechanism of pairing is convincingly electron-phonon coupling (EPC),Duan2014; Ma2014; Papa2015; errea2015; jose2016; bernstein2015; akashi2015; Quan2016, and several predictions of HTS in numerous other metal hydrides at high pressure have appeared, see for example Ma2014; Duan2017; TeHtheory; RHtheory; LaH10theory; eva1; eva2 but relatively little has been decided about the relative importance of the few underlying characteristics that determine Tc. This issue of analysis and understanding of the microscopic mechanisms is the topic of this paper.

For EPC superconductivity, the critical temperature Tc is determined by a retarded Coulomb repulsion μ∗\mu^{\ast}, a minor property that varies only within the range 0.10-0.15, and the function of primary interest, the Eliashberg EPC spectral function α2​F​(ω)=α2​(ω)​F​(ω)\alpha^{2}F(\omega)=\alpha^{2}(\omega)F(\omega), where F⁡(ω)F(\omega) is the phonon density of states (pDOS) and α2​(ω)\alpha^{2}(\omega) gives the coupling strength from phonons of frequency ω\omega. While calculating (or measuring) α2​F\alpha^{2}F is essential for any basic understanding of the coupling, Tc itself can be obtained sufficiently accurately from the Allen-Dynes equationalldyn Tc =T(λ,ωl​o​g,ω2;μ∗)cA​D{}^{AD}_{c}(\lambda,\omega_{log},\omega_{2};\mu^{*}) in terms of the EPC coupling strength λ\lambda and two frequency moments obtained from α2​F\alpha^{2}F, the logarithmic ωl​o​g\omega_{log} and second ω2\omega_{2} frequency moments. II Atomic Analysis: H versus metal atom X

Binary hydrides provide a unique opportunity: the light mass of H results in separation of the phonon spectrum ωq,ν\omega_{q,\nu} and F⁡(ω)F(\omega) into disjoint metal atom low frequency, and H high frequency, regimes, with examples given below. One thus obtains separate α2​Fj​(ω)\alpha^{2}F_{j}(\omega) functions for each atom type jj=X or H from the associated frequency regime, and consequently for λ=λX+λH\lambda=\lambda_{X}+\lambda_{H} as well. This separation provides the extension to specific atomic contributions λj\lambda_{j} at the right side of Eq. 1. The subscript refers to each atom type jj; Nj​(0)N_{j}(0) is the atom-type projected electronic DOS and the other quantities are evident. Since the denominator and Nj​(0)N_{j}(0) are known after calculation, we can for the first time extract the atom-specific Fermi surface averaged matrix elements Ij2I_{j}^{2} for each atom type. Specifically, we obtain IH2I_{H}^{2} for each hydride for comparison. In addition, H frequency moments that are uncontaminated by XX contributions are obtained, for comparison across the hydrides.

We first note that from Eq. 3 it seems crucial for high pressure superconductivity that Ij2I_{j}^{2} increases with pressure comparably to Mj​ω2,j2M_{j}\omega_{2,j}^{2}, to maintain if not to increase λj\lambda_{j} and λ\lambda. The behavior of the atom specific I2I^{2} in compounds is almost unexplored, the exception being some insight obtained from the rigid atomic potential model,GG which has been applied successfully to close packed medium temperature (former high temperature) superconductors. While all the contributions to Ij2I_{j}^{2} are available from modern EPC codes, the information has never been extracted and exploited for a deeper understanding of screening of the proton and impact on high Tc.

The importance of I2I^{2} is evident as it is one of the three components of λj=ηj/κj\lambda_{j}=\eta_{j}/\kappa_{j}: Nj​(0)N_{j}(0), Ij2I_{j}^{2}, and Mj​ω2,j2≡κjM_{j}\omega_{2,j}^{2}\equiv\kappa_{j}. κj\kappa_{j} is the effective harmonic lattice stiffness constant for atom jj, thus the McMillan-HopfieldMcMillan; Hopfield parameter ηj=Nj​(0)​Ij2\eta_{j}=N_{j}(0)I_{j}^{2} is an effective electronic stiffness for atom jj, and λj=ηj/κj\lambda_{j}=\eta_{j}/\kappa_{j} is their ratio. The strong coupling limit explored by Allen and Dynesalldyn gives Tc→0.18​η/M=0.18​N⁡(0)​I2/MT_{c}\rightarrow 0.18\sqrt{\eta/M}=0.18\sqrt{N(0)I^{2}/M}, further emphasizing the importance of I2I^{2} along with N⁡(0)N(0) and MM, also indicating the seeming irrelevance of frequencies. For these hydrides, we obtain a linear relation between Tc and H (not total) parameters, discussed later.

III Crystal structure and methods

In Fig. 1 the bcc I​m​3¯​mIm{\bar{3}}m space group structure of SH3, the fcc F​m​3¯​mFm{\bar{3}}m structure of LaH10, which has two hydrogen sites H2 (green) and H8 (blue) with two and eight sites respectively, and the bcc XXH6 structure are shown. (For structural information see the SM.) An overview of the electronic band structure and atom-projected density of states (DOS) are also shown. Several bands cross the Fermi energy (the zero of energy) so the detailed band structure per se provides little useful information about superconductivity. The LaH10 1:10 stoichiometry is calculated to be dynamically stable in the observed pressure range and the La sublattice has been observed to be fcc,MS2019 making it the candidate structure of choice for the recent signals of superconductivity in La-H samples in the 250-280K range.ME2018a; MS2019; ME2018b From one viewpoint, the La atom sits inside a hydrogen cage of 32 H atoms, as shown in Fig. 1, prompting the description as a clathrate structure.

Electronic structure calculations were carried out using the pseudopotential (PP) Quantum Espresso (QE) code.QE We have found that the results can be sensitive to the choice of PP, which partially accounts for the differing results that can be found in the literature for certain compounds. We have conservatively and consistently used Hamann’s optimized norm-conserved Vanderbilt PPs identified as o​n​c​voncv.Hamann The energy cutoffs for wave function and charge density expansion are 80 Ry and 480 Ry respectively.

For self-consistent calculations, a mesh of 24×\times24×\times24 k points is used. The generalized gradient approximationGGA was adopted for the exchange-correlation functional. The optimized tetrahedron method, as implemented by Kawamura et al. in QE, is used for Brillouin zone integration.kawamura The dense mesh that we have used provides accurate energy resolutions of N⁡(E)N(E) when van Hove singularities fall at EFE_{F}, as occurs in both SH3 and LaH10. For phonon dispersion calculations, the 6×\times6×\times6 q-mesh includes the Γ\Gamma point, while to obtain electron-phonon coupling from the optimized tetrahedron method, we used a similar mesh that is displaced from Γ\Gamma.

Anharmonic corrections are known to be important for phonons and thereby Tc in SH3, and to stabilize it to lower pressures.errea2015 Quantum fluctuations of the H atom arise in SH3akashi2015 and can shift boundaries in the phase diagrams.errea2016 In this study we restrict ourselves to the harmonic approximation and neglect quantum fluctuations; these effects shift phase diagram boundaries but do not impact our conclusions. Only with these simplifications do the formal expressions for EPC apply. Tc is calculated consistently for all compounds from the full Allen-Dynes equation, which is a refitting to dozens of calculations to an extension of the McMillan equation for Tc to include (very) strong coupling and phonon-spectrum-shape corrections. The full expression, which sports a prefactor of the logarithmic moment ωl​o​g\omega_{log} as a primary feature, is provided in the SM.

IV New behavior holding across the hydrides

The compounds we discuss – SH3; CaH6 and MgH6; LaH10 and YH10 – share broad features: they have cubic symmetry, they have a single XX atom per primitive cell, and many bands cross EFE_{F} (see Fig. 1 for crystal structures and band structures), giving a multisheeted Fermi surface, the details of which do not seem to be important except for the possible occurrence of vHs.Quan2016 At the high pressures, lying variously across 160-400 GPa across this study, for which these structures have been reported (calculated) to be harmonically stable, the H vibrations dominate the optic modes with energies up to 220-250 meV, which are distinct from the X dominated acoustic modes at 70 meV or lower, depending on the XX atom mass. Tables I-III in the Appendix contains the materials parameters obtained from our studies. The main results are as follows.

IV.1 The dominance of hydrogen

The anticipated importance of H for TcT_{c} in hydrides is clouded by the observation that the XX atom provides 15-25% of λ\lambda, seemingly very important. An overriding feature in our results of the tables in the Appendix is that coupling λX\lambda_{X} from the metal atom is useless in increasing Tc, at best enhancing Tc by only 3% although the total λ\lambda is increased by the above mentioned 15-25%. More startlingly, including the XX portion of α2​F\alpha^{2}F can decrease Tc. For example, for LaH10 at both 250 and 300 GPa, including λX\lambda_{X} increases λ\lambda by +14%, but this increased strength at low frequency decreases ωl​o​g\omega_{log} by 18% producing a net decrease of Tc by 5%. λX\lambda_{X} thus becomes a source of misconceptions, and by being included in obtaining Tc as in previous calculations, it has resulted in an impression (incorrect) that it contributes proportionally to Tc.

This anti-intuitive behavior appears to contradict the result of Bergmann and RainerRainer that any small increase in coupling increases Tc, that is, δ\deltaTc/δ​α2\delta\alpha^{2}F(OPENω)\omega) is non-negative. The resolution of this conundrum lies in effects that have been addressed before:pbamlc1972; pba1972; wep2008 in physical materials (and in a self-consistent treatment) an increase in α2​F\alpha^{2}F at a given frequency will feed back into a softening of phonon modes. This mode softening always opposes the positive effect on Tc from the increase in λ\lambda. For XX=La in LaH10 the softening dominates, and (as mentioned) Tc drops by 5% in spite of stronger coupling, just before the lattice instability sets in (see below). Tc in CaH6 and MgH6 is effectively unchanged under the 15-20% increase from λX\lambda_{X}; SH3 shows a small positive effect. The important message is that for Tc, λX\lambda_{X} is ineffectual and it should be disregarded to gain knowledge about increasing Tc. This option is included in the tables in the Appendix.

IV.2 Our major results

Since it was just established that XX atom coupling is ineffective at best and misleading in practice, henceforward we focus on the H atom contributions alone: unless otherwise stated (sometimes the H subscript is included for emphasis), our comments apply only to the H atoms’ contributions (the rows in the tables in the Appendix labeled “H”). The following observations are drawn from the ω2−Tc\omega_{2}-T_{c} phase diagram and six other panels providing a variety of correlations in Fig. 2.

IV.2.1 High frequencies are important

Higher Tc compounds have higher frequency moments, as shown in Fig. 2(a) and (e). Figure 2(a) provides an ω2\omega_{2} vs. TcT_{c} phase diagram, which identifies a boundary separating the high Tc region from an island of lattice instability. Interestingly, MgH6 at the highest Tc end has very similar frequencies as SH3. Since the denominator MH​ω22M_{H}\omega_{2}^{2} in λ\lambda is the same for these two materials, the numerator η\eta must be substantially larger. Figure 2(f) and the tables in the Appendix indeed show that η\eta is ∼\sim35% larger, with twice as large matrix elements IH2I_{H}^{2} [Fig. 2(g)] overcoming a somewhat lower value of N⁡(0)N(0). This is the first clear evidence of a strong material dependence of IH2I_{H}^{2} in hydrides.

IV.2.2 Strongest coupling involves lower frequencies

Since pressure enhancement of hydride Tc has been a prevalent notion, we quantify that Tc decreases with increasing pressure and increasing frequencies within each class studied. Strong coupling is (unfortunately) associated with lower frequencies, within a region of stability. This result (noted previously in some individual materials) seems in opposition to conventional wisdom that higher pressure is better for Tc. Our results establish that Tc is maximum at the lower pressure end of crystal stability where frequencies are softer, as shown in Fig. 2(e). Tc is ultimately limited in these systems, as in many strong coupled but lower Tc analogues, by lattice instabilityWeber1973; Testardi1975; Pickett2006 that depends on the details of EPC of the material. The emerging picture is that while pressure stabilizes favorable structures with metallic atomic H, providing high Tc with high frequencies, within each phase additional pressure increases frequencies and lowers Tc. To repeat: the essential role of pressure is simply to stabilize structures composed of atomic H; further pressure is detrimental for Tc. Less pressure, that is, the instability region, comprises insulating phases with H2 and H- units, or conducting structures with these units,eva which do not promote strong scattering and strong EPC.

IV.2.3 H matrix elements are not “atomic properties”

The derived squared H matrix element IH2I_{H}^{2} has been suggested to be an “atomic quantity,”Hopfield; Ashcroft not varying much from material to material. IH2I_{H}^{2} is highlighted in boldface in Tables I-III of the Appendix and plotted versus pressure in Fig. 2(g), facilitating observing that it differs by a factor of five for these compounds: 24 for LaH10 to 125 in MgH6, each in eV2/Å2. Evidently the screening of the proton displacement is sensitive to the response of the environment, and IH2I_{H}^{2} is not the “atomic quantity” as earlier suggested.

IV.2.4 Impact of atomic fraction of H

Is the atomic fraction of H a crucial factor? By dividing NH​(0)N_{H}(0) in the tables in the Appendix by the number of H atoms, the contribution per H atom is obtained. The values range from around 0.022 for CaH6 and MgH6 to 0.033 for the vHs compounds SH3, LaH10, and YH10; units are states/(eV atom spin). These values, which represent chemical differences and can be sensitive to the precision of the calculation and to decompositions into XX and H contributions, do not scale well with Tc.

IV.2.5 Behavior of λ\lambda(P)

The variation of λ\lambda with pressure depends primarily on the strong variation with pressure of the lattice stiffness κ=M​ω22\kappa=M\omega_{2}^{2}, see Fig. 2(c). For example, κ\kappa decreases by 55% in CaH6 from 300 to 150 GPa, beyond which the lattice becomes unstable. The minor variation of the electronic stiffness η=N⁡(0)​I2\eta=N(0)I^{2} is apparent from Fig. 2 (f). Increasing λ\lambda by softening the lattice increases Tc for currently studied hydrides but encounters lattice instability for λH≈\lambda_{H}\approx2.2.

IV.2.6 Achievement of “atomic hydrogen”

These alkaline earth and rare earth based compounds are effectively atomic hydrogen crystals with a charge carrier void (more precisely, a scattering strength void) in the volume consumed by the XX atom: the XX atom serves to compress and to provide bonding to hydrogen to produce atomic (versus diatomic) H, thereby producing HTS. This observation suggests that element(s) that are most able to “break” H2 molecules into atoms in a crystalline environment provide the most promise of providing Ashcroft’s “chemical precompression concept,”Ashcroft i.e. decreasing the pressure necessary to obtain HTS hydrides.

V Prospects for Higher Tc, and Limitations

We collect here some important characteristics, by example from the various compounds.

V.1 Strong coupling and lattice instability

There have been many examples over several decadesWeber1973; Testardi1975; Pickett2006 where pushing a superconducting system toward stronger coupling results in marginally higher Tc, accompanied by renormalization toward softer phonons followed by lattice instability. The process is understood: EPC renormalizes phonon frequencies downward from their bare values Ωq\Omega_{q}:

ωq2=Ωq2−2​Ωq​Πq​(ωq)\displaystyle\omega_{q}^{2}=\Omega_{q}^{2}-2\Omega_{q}\Pi_{q}(\omega_{q}) (4)

where Πq​(ω)\Pi_{q}(\omega) is the phonon self-energy that increases with λq\lambda_{q}: increasing coupling drives frequencies downward, as seen from the tables in the Appendix. Then, lower frequencies increase the coupling strength measured by λ\lambda (other things being equal): it is a cooperative process inviting vanishing frequencies and the accompanying lattice instability.

The process is illustrated for SH3 in Fig. 3, where F⁡(ω),α2​F​(ω),α2​F​(ω)/ωF(\omega),\alpha^{2}F(\omega),\alpha^{2}F(\omega)/\omega, and α2​(ω)\alpha^{2}(\omega) are shown for a range of harmonic lattice stability above the instability around 140 GPa, from which distinct features can be identified. The differences with pressure in F⁡(ω)F(\omega) are unexceptional, with some hardening of the high frequency H modes proceeding as expected. Differences in α2​F\alpha^{2}F begin to be more evident: peak values decrease from 170 to 150 to 130 meV as pressure is lowered. This shift downward of coupling strength is more striking in α2​(ω)=α2​F​(ω)/F⁡(ω)\alpha^{2}(\omega)=\alpha^{2}F(\omega)/F(\omega), which reveals very strong coupling in the 80-120 meV region. These H-derived optic modes are reflected in the moments of α2​F\alpha^{2}F in Tables in the Appendix: ω2\omega_{2} decreases by a third before instability. Neither the moments – e.g. ωl​o​g\omega_{log}, which (over)emphasizes the low frequency modes – nor λ\lambda dictates the instability of the lattice by vanishing or diverging, respectively.

Instead, a single branch (with small phase space) dips toward zero and the structure becomes dynamically unstable. In these hydrides the lower pressure, roomier structures tend to allow molecular-like dimerization of some of the H atoms into H2 molecules, which is unfavorable for metallicity and strong coupling. Figure 4 indicates the regions of the zone where instabilities in SH3 and LaH10 occur. In HS3 the instability lies along the H-P symmetry line along the zone boundary, with another instability occurring at Γ\Gamma. In LaH10 the instabilities occur in a donut shape centered on the L point. In both cases as well as in CaH6, the instability involves wavevectors at or near the zone boundaries, with the short wavelengths being suggestive of the instabilities being related to H2 molecule type fluctuation and formation.

V.2 Highest Tc class: rare earth decahydrides XXH10

A noteworthy feature is that, for LaH10 as in SH3 which are the two materials so far observed to be superconducting nearing room temperature, the Fermi level accidentally (if it is accidental) falls between the energies of a pair of closely spaced van Hove singularities (vHs). The associated pieces of Fermi surface in LaH10 and resulting vHs peak in N(E) involve solely the H8 site, see Fig. 1. The additional physicsQuan2016 occurring in SH3 due to vHs will also apply to LaH10 (but in less prominent form), but that is not the topic of this paper. The variation of N⁡(E)N(E) from 210 GPa to 300 GPa, is regular but minor, and the changes in Fermi surface are not visible in surface plots. YH10 has a predicted Tc=250-270K compared to LaH10 at 200-215K. The values of λ\lambda at the two pressures studied are the same; the difference lies in the ∼40\sim 40% higher value of η=N⁡(0)​I2\eta=N(0)I^{2} in YH10, where a much larger value of I2I^{2} overcomes a somewhat lower value of N⁡(0)N(0). Based on current examples, increasing λ\lambda near the instability by phonon softening does increase Tc but also drives the instability, a familiar story from 1970s materials.

V.3 Variations within a class: alkaline earth hexahydrides XXH6

Metal hexahydrides have been predicted to include high Tc superconductors at high pressure, but synthesis and study of their properties have not yet been reported. Given the regularities discussed above, it is eye-opening to note that both the lowest and the highest Tc members in Fig. 2 are CaH6 and MgH6 respectively, despite being isostructural, isovalent, and in neighboring rows in the periodic table. The difference, surprisingly, is not in higher frequencies in the smaller cell (the frequencies are similar) but in the matrix elements IH2I_{H}^{2}. The origin of this difference is a topic of ongoing study for us.

A plot of the H-related parameters for CaH6 at 150-300 GPa in Fig. 5, normalized to their values at 300 GPa, illuminate relative increases and decreases with pressure. The main trends follow those of SH3: Tc is highest at the lower pressure, with a quick upturn in λ\lambda and Tc just before the lattice becomes unstable. For this structure as for others: once the structure becomes stable, Tc(P) decreases with increasing pressure, by a factor of two in our range of study.

VI Areas for focus

VI.1 ηH\eta_{H} versus κH\kappa_{H}

The hydrides studied here reach their maximum Tc, just before instability, near λH≈\lambda_{H}\approx 2.2 (somewhat smaller in SH3), while Tc varies from 200K to 285K. The distinction is that those with higher mean frequency just above the instability have the higher Tc. At first glance, the goal should be to retain strong coupling at the higher frequencies; for room temperature Tc Bergmann and Rainer’s analysisRainer suggests that for a 300K superconductor, coupling at 2​π​kB​Tc2\pi k_{B}T_{c}=165 meV and above is optimal; this is in the range of the mean frequencies of the highest Tc hydrides (see tables in Appendix). This choice of goal is somewhat simplistic, however, because the strongly coupled low frequency modes are in the lower frequency (not optimal frequency) range and are approaching instability precisely because they are the most strongly coupled (a chicken and egg relationship). The best strategy seems to be to (somehow) retain strong coupling as evenly as possible over all H vibrations, preferably utilizing all momenta. Such a scenario avoids a lattice instability until a large fraction of modes become soft.

This brings consideration to the McMillan-Hopfield constant ηH=NH​(0)​IH2\eta_{H}=N_{H}(0)I_{H}^{2}, which the analysis of Allen and Dynes indicates as the limiting behavior of Tc at large coupling. Fig. 2(f) shows that ηH\eta_{H} is much larger for the higher Tc materials (LaH10 is an exception). The next challenge therefore is to engineer ηH\eta_{H} because (i) so little is known about how to maximize the matrix elements I2I^{2}, and (ii) N⁡(0)N(0) can be sensitive to details of band structure that simply has to be calculated. Gaining an understanding of H scattering IH2I_{H}^{2} is a current challenge but a realistic one, and one that will be crucial in learning how to retain coupling over as many H branches as possible.

VI.2 Leavens-Carbotte analysis

An understanding of how to increase Tc requires one to internalize the actual factors that determine Tc at this time, which is not in the strong coupling limit. In this respect: the somewhat involved Allen-Dynes expression is opaque – despite its appearance, it is not exponential at all. Leavens and Carbotte found for strong coupling materials of the time (1974) leavens that the area AA under α2​F\alpha^{2}F, which from the various definitions is A=λ​ω1/2A=\lambda\omega_{1}/2, was a faithful indicator of Tc: Tc≈0.148​AT_{c}\approx 0.148A. Using our H-based values of AA and Tc, their relationship is presented in Fig. 6, along with the Leavens and Carbotte slope of 0.148. The agreement for these five hydrides is stunningly close to their value; a least squares fit to Tc=S A +To gives a practically equivalent slope of SS=0.150 and a small intercept of To=-6K – a direct relationship to within computational uncertainty. This relationship focuses the challenge: maximize the product λ​ω1\lambda\omega_{1}. Allen and Dynes proved that the strong coupling limit of Eliashberg theory is Tc∝λω2{}_{c}\propto\sqrt{\lambda}\omega_{2} (note: the difference between ω1\omega_{1} and ω2\omega_{2} in these hydrides is a nearly constant ratio, so for these purposes they may be considered interchangeable). Thus the strong coupling regime in hydrides has not been reached, and the Leavens-Carbotte expression provides the quantity to focus on increasing.

Our work provides another guide for reducing the pressure required for HTS hydrides. One objective is to find the element(s) XX in XXHn that serves to disassociate the H2 unit into atomic H in the lattice at the lowest possible pressure. Many examples indicate that a high Tc phase is then likely to emerge. Our view then is that the optimum set of materials parameters, for higher Tc possibly at lower pressures, is yet to be achieved.
